# The Meridian Telescope

The Meridian Telescope is a 3.2 m telescope that has operated on Aurora Ridge
since 2004. Its primary mirror is segmented: 36 hexagonal segments, each about
0.55 m across, made of borosilicate glass and coated with a thin layer of
aluminum. The segments together form a single 3.2 m aperture. Active optics
adjust the position of every segment twice per minute, using measurements from
a wavefront sensor, so that the mirror keeps its shape as temperature and
pointing change.

Cleaning the mirror is a careful process. The primary mirror is made of 36
borosilicate glass segments, and every 18 months the observatory cleans them
with carbon dioxide snow, often written as CO2 snow. A nozzle directs a stream
of dry CO2 snow across each coated surface, lifting dust and pollen without
touching the glass or leaving a solvent residue. The observatory does not use
liquid cleaners on the primary mirror. The aluminum coating is renewed every
five years, and the recoating is scheduled during the winter closure.

Three facility instruments are mounted on the telescope: the ARCFIELD camera, the
POLARIS spectrograph, and the GLINT near-infrared imager. ARCFIELD is a wide-field
optical camera with a 0.8 degree by 0.8 degree field of view, used for surveys
and variable-star monitoring. POLARIS is a high-resolution spectrograph with a
resolving power of about 120,000; it sits in a climate-controlled room below
the telescope to keep its measurements stable. GLINT works with the adaptive
optics system. Observers normally change instruments every three to four
months.

The adaptive optics system uses a deformable mirror with 189 actuators and a
laser guide star. Under good conditions it sharpens near-infrared images to
about 0.05 arcseconds, compared with the site's natural seeing of roughly 1.1
arcseconds. The system works best when the air is stable and the guide star is
bright, and the observing team decides at the start of each night whether to
use it.

Meridian is mounted on an altitude-azimuth mount and weighs about 120 tonnes,
including the mirror cell. The enclosure is a classical dome with a wide slit;
ventilation doors on the lower level help the dome follow the outside
temperature in the late afternoon. The telescope is controlled from an
operations room on the second floor, and a second console in Lake Harrow can
take over for remote observing.

The telescope's first light image was of the spiral galaxy NGC 4051, a target
chosen mainly because it was well placed on the commissioning night. That image
is still printed on the wall of the control room.
