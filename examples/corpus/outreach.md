# Outreach and Dark-Sky Policy

Aurora Ridge Observatory runs a citizen science program called NightWatch,
started in 2019. About 300 active volunteers classify variable-star light
curves, check candidate transits, and flag artifacts in survey images. New
volunteers attend a training weekend in April and then work through a set of
practice datasets before joining a project. The minimum age is 16, and no
previous astronomy experience is required. Volunteers are credited in the
annual data release notes when their classifications contribute to a published
result.

Schools join the citizen science program through the NightWatch Classroom
track by submitting an application by March 1 for the fall semester or by
October 1 for the spring semester. The track is open to grades 6 through 12,
with groups of 10 to 30 students. Priority goes to schools within 150 km of
Lake Harrow, although schools farther away can join the remote program. The
program is free and includes a teacher workshop, classroom data activities, and
one remote observing session on the 0.6 m Lumen Telescope.

Public visits and school visits are kept separate. The Saturday visitor gallery
tours from May through September are open to individual visitors and families,
with a maximum of 40 people per tour. School groups use the classroom track and
do not book the public tour slots. The observatory also hosts an open house on
the third Saturday of August, when the visitor gallery, the control room, and
the machine shop are open to the public.

The observatory's dark-sky policy is one of the strictest in the region. It
maintains a dark-sky zone extending 40 km from the summit. The dark-sky policy
requires outdoor lighting at the site to use full-cutoff, shielded fixtures and
limits LED color temperature to 3000 K or lower. Decorative lighting is turned
off after 22:00, and light trespass beyond property lines is limited to 0.5 lux.
The observatory works with Lake Harrow and the surrounding county on lighting
rules and offers a small rebate to residents who replace unshielded fixtures.

The policy is not only about the telescope. Staff argue that dark skies are part
of the ridge's character and that good lighting also saves energy and helps
nocturnal wildlife. Each year the outreach team gives talks to the town council
and publishes a short report on sky brightness measured at three sites around
the ridge. The measurements are made on moonless nights and compared with
readings from the previous year.
