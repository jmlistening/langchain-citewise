# Observatory Operations

The observatory schedules about 210 observing nights per year. Roughly 160 of
those nights are usable; the rest are lost to weather, instrument work, or
engineering time. Telescope time is allocated twice a year by the consortium
Time Allocation Committee: about 70 percent of the nights go to consortium
programs, 20 percent to external researchers, and 10 percent to the director's
discretionary pool. Long programs are reviewed together with short programs so
that survey work and single-night targets compete on the same basis.

During the annual winter closure, from January 15 to February 28, deep snow and
avalanche risk make the access road unsafe, and the site receives only
maintenance visits by snow tractor. Staff use the closure for instrument
upgrades, software work, and the five-year recoating of the primary mirror when
it is due. No science observing and no public tours take place during this
period. The road usually reopens in early March, and the first observing run of
the year is scheduled for the second week of March.

Observing stops when weather conditions exceed these limits: wind gusts above
45 km/h, relative humidity above 85 percent, the temperature below -25 degrees
Celsius, any precipitation, or lightning within 10 km. The telescope operator
and two observers on duty monitor these limits continuously and close the dome
when a limit is reached. Seeing worse than 2.5 arcseconds also stops science
exposures, because the data quality would be too poor to justify the wear on
the instruments.

The data pipeline is called AURORA. Raw frames reach the archive within 10
minutes of readout, and calibrated frames are normally available within 6
hours. The archive holds about 480 TB and grows by roughly 60 TB per year.
Consortium members can access calibration data immediately; science data are
released to the consortium after 12 months and to the public after 24 months.
Each raw file keeps a checksum, and the pipeline records the software version
used for every reduction so that results can be reproduced later.

Staffing follows a simple pattern. One telescope operator runs the dome and the
telescope, two observers manage the instrument and the queue, and a remote
support astronomer is available by video link from Lake Harrow. The operations
team publishes a weekly schedule on Monday, and changes caused by weather are
logged in the same system. Every closure is recorded with the reason and the
time, which helps the consortium track weather losses over the year.
